Noticing in Online Turkish Learning

Trinity College Dublin Hilary Term 2026 · CALL foundations

Summary: This post connects the Noticing Hypothesis and corrective feedback research to design choices in online Turkish-learning activities.

How the Noticing Hypothesis Is Applied in Online Turkish Learning

The Noticing Hypothesis was first introduced by Richard Schmidt in 1990, and it states that in order for acquisition to take place, learners need to notice the features of the language in the input. The Noticing Hypothesis states that attention regulates awareness, and only what is noticed can be intake. The Noticing Hypothesis has been one of the most influential theories in Second Language Acquisition in the past decades.

In Leow’s introduction to the Noticing Hypothesis, he highlights that second language learners are limited-capacity processors who cannot process all the input they receive simultaneously. Leow states that the process of noticing is the first step in the learning process, where linguistic items are processed in working memory before being stored in long-term memory. It is essential to note that Leow also states that Schmidt later modified his original claim and stated that the more an individual notices, the more they learn, thus emphasizing the role of noticing as a facilitative condition rather than a sufficient condition for language acquisition. Moreover, Leow states that greater processing and awareness lead to better learning outcomes.

This cognitive approach is supported by the review of Noticing in Second Language Acquisition by Zhang (2022),, which examines the role of attention and awareness in interlanguage development. Zhang argues that noticing is a vital level of consciousness that mediates input and long-term memory. The study has also pointed out some variables that can affect noticing, such as perceptual salience, task complexity, instruction, and processing capacity of learners. Zhang concludes that learners will typically acquire what they attend to, and learning environments that draw attention to form contribute greatly to second language development.

Empirical evidence for noticing can be found in the area of corrective feedback. In the study “Explicit vs. Implicit Corrective Feedback: Which is More Effective?” (2022) , Yu shows that explicit corrective feedback results in greater improvements in grammatical awareness, speaking development, and willingness to communicate than implicit corrective feedback. In explicit corrective feedback, learners’ attention is explicitly drawn to the error by correction and metalinguistic comments, which highlights the gap in the language. The study claims that this conscious comparison between the incorrect and correct forms enhances noticing.

These theoretical and empirical results are in good alignment with contemporary CALL systems, such as TurkishClass101 , a web-based tool developed specifically for Turkish language learners. TurkishClass101 offers learners structured input in the form of conversations and transcripts, which are then followed up by explicit grammar explanations related to Turkish particularities like case suffixes, verb conjugation, vowel harmony, and agglutinative morphology. Crucially, the system includes exercises that provide immediate corrective feedback. When learners enter incorrect sentences, such as Ben Ankara gitmek (I.1SG Ankara go-INF), they are presented with the corrected sentence Ben Ankara’ya gidiyorum (I.1SG Ankara-DAT go-PROG-1SG), highlighting both the directional suffix and verb tense. This provides a strong noticing contrast, as hypothesized by Schmidt, and is consistent with the findings of Yu on explicit feedback. Moreover, TurkishClass101 provides spaced repetition and review exercises that return learners’ attention to previously noticed forms, promoting further processing, as outlined by Leow and Zhang.
